# DadCraft's taste notes

What DadCraft's own production work teaches Maestro: four production logs of synthwave covers of video game music
(made with Suno and mixed in Ableton) and his Suno Knowledge Base (github.com/dadcraftttv/Suno-Knowledge-Base).
Read Oct 23. The logs are his and stay private; only these lessons, in our words, are kept here.

**What they are for:** a written source for the style book (bucket 3), above all the synthwave entry and the blends,
and checks to run before DadCraft listens. **What they are not:** data. The audio is AI-made, and covers of game
music are copyrighted compositions (decision 7). Many verdicts were on Suno output before mixing, so they describe
how a style should feel, not exact numbers.

## Blending (decision 4)
- **One style leads, the others season it.** In his best blends the main genre comes first and a co-genre adds one
  trait (darksynth as a third flavour, for menace). In Maestro: one style weight is clearly the biggest, the rest
  small, unless the user asks for an even mix.
- **Each style lends one clear trait:**
  - future bass: bounce and sub weight
  - darksynth: grit and menace
  - trap: rolling hi-hats and a half-time feel
  - synthpop: brightness, a jaunty feel
  - outrun: forward motion
  - chillwave: float and warmth
- **Styles too far apart don't blend in one part.** He layered them as separate parts instead (synthmetal and trap,
  generated apart). That is Maestro's band: trap drums under a synthwave bassist, each member keeping its own style.
- **Some styles fight some moods.** Trap worked for dread only with a driving mood; with chill or meandering
  material it failed. Darksynth was wrong for comic or playful music, and too cold on its own for a bright tropical
  zone. A style's entry should say which energies and moods it suits.

## Synthwave as he hears it
- **Tempo by mood:** calm about 60–100 BPM, menace about 120–140, boss or fanfare about 135–170. Half-time is easy
  to mishear, so the felt tempo matters more than the number.
- **Bass carries the groove** in every zone. Three ways it plays:
  - locked to the kick, for bright zones
  - accents on 2 and 4, for darker, swaggering ones
  - a clean sub under melodic tracks
- **Drums:** a motorik, drum-forward pulse for mechanical threat; trap hats with a half-time feel for dread.
- **Pads:** supersaw pads stand in for an orchestra. Chillwave pads float.
- **Arps:** a high sparkle arp for glint; cold new-wave arps for icy dread.
- **Mode:** minor gives urgency; major can turn "too hopeful" for a dark target.
- **Energy:** heaviness grows through a section ("Music gets heavier as zone progresses"). Fast tracks start at full
  energy from the first beat; slow swells suit calm openings only.

## Failures to check for (before DadCraft listens)
- **The melody gets lost:** the melody must stay on top, with the rest of the band thinner or lower under it.
- **Two moods at once turn to mush.** One mood per section; change mood at section changes.
- **Too fast or too bombastic** for a calm moment; menace that "hits too hard and fast".
- **Too bright** for a section that should be darkening.
- **The key drifts** toward the wrong mode.
- **Mud:** too many parts in the low mids at once. For Maestro: low parts' registers and density kept apart.

## How it plays vs what it plays on (decision 3)
He keeps these as separate knobs, as Maestro does.
- **How it plays:** tempo and feel (half-time or full), rhythm (backbeat, syncopation), mode, energy arc (immediate
  or swelling), loop or developing, mood.
- **What it plays on:** supersaw, chillwave pad, sparkle arp, synth organ, synth brass, bells and chimes, music
  box, 808, distorted guitar, marimba, congas.

These sound names are candidates for each style's default sounds (bucket 3) and for Ouralia's kits (bucket 6).
